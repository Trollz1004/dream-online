## Funding a game before there's a game to sell

DREAM Online doesn't have a launch date, a price, or a store page. What it has is a public repository, a set of design pillars, and a page on [Open Collective](https://opencollective.com/dream-online) where anyone can back the project directly, ahead of alpha, ahead of a finished build, ahead of any of the usual reasons a game asks for money.

That ordering is deliberate. Backing this early isn't a pre-order for a product — there isn't one yet. It's closer to putting resources behind a direction you can already see in the public design docs and want to see followed through. The repository shows you exactly what's being built before you decide whether to support it.

## Why Open Collective instead of a typical crowdfunding platform

Open Collective is built around transparency by default — contributions and how they're used are visible on the platform, not summarized after the fact in a wrap-up post. For a project that's already committed to building in the open on GitHub, using a funding platform with the same transparency built in was the obvious fit rather than a stretch.

The Collective sits alongside the same public work you'd find in [the repository](https://github.com/Trollz1004/dream-online) and on [dream-online.net](https://dream-online.net) — it's not a separate, more polished storefront. It's the same project, with a way to support it directly.

### The four tiers

Four backer tiers are live on the Collective page right now:

- **Backer** — a flexible monthly contribution, starting at $5.
- **Founder** — a one-time contribution at $25.
- **Sponsor** — a monthly contribution starting at $100.
- **Guild** — a one-time contribution at $2,000, capped to a limited number of backers.

Each tier is built around a different way of taking part, from a low-commitment monthly show of support to a larger, capped contribution for backers who want a bigger stake in the project. Exactly what each tier includes is listed on [the Open Collective page itself](https://opencollective.com/dream-online), which is the page to check for the current, complete details — tier benefits are still being finalized and the Collective is the source of truth for what's confirmed as of any given day.

## What backing actually funds

There's no finished game behind this yet, which means backing goes toward the actual work happening right now: the design documents in the repository, the prototypes being built and tested, and the time it takes to get systems like combat, life skills, and world events right before committing to them at scale. It's support for a process that's visible, not a purchase of a result that isn't finished.

### Why tiers instead of a single "support us" button

A single flat contribution option would be simpler to set up, but it treats a $5 supporter and a $2,000 backer as making the same kind of decision, which they clearly aren't. Setting up four separate tiers lets someone commit at whatever level actually fits how invested they are in the project, from a small recurring show of support to a larger, one-time stake that's deliberately capped rather than open to everyone.

That cap on the top tier is worth noting on its own. Guild is limited to a fixed number of backers, which means it isn't just "the expensive tier" — it's structured as a smaller group within the larger backer base. Details of what that group gets are still being finalized on the Collective page, but the limit itself is already part of the design, not an afterthought added later.

### What deciding on a tier actually looks like

If you're weighing which tier makes sense, the honest framing is less about what you'll receive back and more about how you want to be involved. A monthly Backer contribution is a low-friction way to stay attached to a project you're watching develop. A one-time Founder contribution suits someone who wants to make a single decision and be done with it. Sponsor and Guild are built for people who want a larger stake, whether that's recurring support at a meaningful level or a one-time contribution into a capped group. None of these are framed as an investment with a financial return — they're contributions to a project whose specific benefits are still being written down.

### What backing is not

Backing DREAM Online isn't pay-to-win, and it isn't a claim on in-game power once the game exists — the project's stated monetization intent, now and later, is that anything sold affects convenience or appearance only, never combat power. Supporting development early doesn't create an exception to that. It also isn't a guarantee of a launch date or a finished feature list; none of those exist yet, and backing doesn't change that timeline on its own.

## Why back a game with no launch date at all

It's a fair question. The straightforward answer is that backing here isn't a bet on a release date — it's a bet on a process you can already inspect. Most crowdfunded games ask you to trust a pitch and a timeline neither of which you can verify. DREAM Online's pitch is the repository itself: the design documents are the same ones the team works from, the commit history is the same one that would exist whether or not anyone was watching, and the pillars on the site are a plain-language summary of that same work. Backing early means supporting that process while it's happening, not waiting for a trailer to decide whether it looks convincing.

## How this connects to the open-development approach

Most of what makes backing this project make sense is the same thing that makes the public repository make sense: you're not being asked to trust a pitch, you're being shown the actual work. The design pillars on [dream-online.net](https://dream-online.net), the commit history on GitHub, and the tier details on Open Collective are all pointing at the same project from different angles. Backing is one more way to engage with development that's already public, rather than a separate transaction layered on top of it.
